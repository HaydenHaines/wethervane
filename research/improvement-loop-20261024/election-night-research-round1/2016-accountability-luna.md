# Round 1: 2016 forecasts, misses, and accountability

## Findings

2016 is useful because several distinct forecast failures and successes coexisted. The national popular-vote polls were close; state polls were materially worse and leaned toward Clinton; forecasts translated those inputs into very different win chances; and election coverage often let a probability sound like a guarantee. A scorecard should keep those judgments separate.

### What was forecast, and what happened

The forecast-time objects were not interchangeable. FiveThirtyEight published polls-only, polls-plus, and a “now-cast”; its methods described state polling, poll adjustments, demographic regression, uncertainty simulations, and cross-state correlated errors. The polls-plus model added economic fundamentals, while the now-cast answered a different question: what would happen if voting were held immediately. This distinction matters when selecting a preferred model and horizon. Its contemporaneous [forecast guide](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/a-users-guide-to-fivethirtyeights-2016-general-election-forecast/) is currently redirected; the [October 31 forecast snapshot](https://archive.ph/fEHf8) preserves the live forecast. A contemporaneous primary data archive and predeclared comparison method are preserved in [BuzzFeed News’s forecast grading repository](https://github.com/BuzzFeedNews/2016-11-grading-the-election-forecasts); it separates win probabilities from expected margin and vote-share forecasts, and notes when the denominators are not comparable.

Princeton Election Consortium (PEC) provides a particularly clear frozen example: on Election Day its mode was Clinton 323 electoral votes, median 307, one-sigma range 281–326, popular-vote estimate Clinton +4.0 ±0.6, and win chance 93%. PEC also explicitly explained in June that its displayed daily state snapshot was not the same as its November win probability: the latter added uncertainty about campaign movement. Its methodology used state polling, a t-distribution, electoral-vote simulations, and a separate movement assumption. But in August, PEC had reduced its estimated campaign volatility from 6% to 3%, increasing Clinton’s estimated win chance (random-drift 78→92%, Bayesian 86→95%) because recent decades seemed more stable. That was a public, testable modeling choice with consequence: the upset should prompt investigation of whether this volatility assumption and state-error model were too narrow, not the blanket claim that every component was invalid. ([PEC final projections](https://election.princeton.edu/articles/final-projections-2016/); [PEC methodology](https://election.princeton.edu/articles/the-presidential-meta-analysis-for-2016/); [August volatility change](https://election.princeton.edu/articles/sharpening-the-2016-presidential-forecast/))

The [AAPOR post-election review](https://aapor.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/AAPOR-2016-Election-Polling-Report.pdf) found final national polls’ average absolute margin error was 2.2 points, with signed error 1.3 points toward Trump; they indicated Clinton’s popular-vote lead reasonably well. State polls’ average absolute error was 5.1 points and signed error 3.0 points toward Trump. Pennsylvania, Michigan, and Wisconsin were called for Clinton by polls but won by Trump, enough to flip the Electoral College. Across the final 13 days, battleground polls did better on average than other state polls, but the battleground error pattern still included the decisive Upper Midwest. Actual state results and forecasts should be compared state by state against a timestamped official canvass, not collapsed into a single “polls wrong” label.

Those are point errors in estimates. They are not alone a calibration test of 70%, 90%, or 99% win probabilities. A single loss after a 70% forecast is compatible with a well-calibrated forecast; a 93% forecast losing is more concerning, but still one Bernoulli result, not proof by itself. Conversely, saying “Trump always had a chance” is inadequate accountability: it does not assess whether the probability was too high, whether the joint state-error distribution assigned enough mass to a coordinated Trump shift, whether its stated margin intervals were useful, or whether similar high-probability forecasts repeatedly lose. Those questions need proper probability scores and repeated, comparable forecast cases, alongside point-margin error and interval coverage/width. State outcomes are not independent replications: common polling error or a national late swing moves multiple states together. Do not count Michigan, Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, and the presidential winner as four or more independent national shocks.

### What the miss can and cannot diagnose

AAPOR found that correlated error is common in a given election, but its partisan direction was not consistent across cycles: polls understated Democrats in 2000 and 2012, and Trump in 2016. It identified plausible 2016 contributors, with caveats: late deciders in MI/WI/PA leaned toward Trump; many state polls did not weight for education despite education being tied to vote choice and survey participation; late-revealing Trump supporters also appeared, while other tests did not support a broad “shy Trump” effect. AAPOR’s report cautions against a single-cause story. It also found state polling quality and methods varied, and its contemporaneous Nov. 9 release documented the immediate “crisis in polling” narrative before evidence was in. Later review concluded that the “polls failed” story was too broad: national polls were historically accurate, while state polls and Electoral College expectations deserved scrutiny. ([AAPOR release and findings](https://www.p2016.org/media/aapor110916pr.html))

For model accountability, distinguish: (1) **event surprise**: Trump won despite favored status; (2) **point-margin error**: expected vote margins differed from results, especially in the Upper Midwest; (3) **correlated errors**: several battleground estimates moved together in one direction; (4) **probability error**: the assigned chance may have been too confident, assessable only against a sufficiently broad, dependence-aware set of forecasts; (5) **mechanism failure**: a model’s stated assumptions, such as the volatility scale or the amount of shared state error, may have omitted plausible variation; and (6) **communication failure**: presenting “90%” without the likely margin, plausible joint outcomes, and a plain-language frequency may make “likely” sound certain.

There is direct evidence for the last issue, though not for a causal claim about the 2016 result. AAPOR documented public backlash/distrust and recommended clearer margin uncertainty and correlated-error communication. A Pew write-up of an experiment found that people shown probability forecasts inferred greater certainty and less competitiveness than people shown equivalent vote-share margins; a second experiment found reduced voting in a simulated election game. The researchers explicitly could not establish that forecast framing changed real 2016 turnout or the election result. This supports an explanation/communication standard, not an accusation that the forecast caused Trump’s victory. ([Pew’s study summary](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2018/02/06/use-of-election-forecasts-in-campaign-coverage-can-confuse-voters-and-may-lower-turnout/); [study authors’ summary](https://www.asc.upenn.edu/news-events/news/probabilistic-forecasting-can-mislead-voters-about-certainty-election-outcomes))

### Lessons for a preferred-model election-night guide

- Freeze a named model, version, forecast time, horizon, result source, and definitions. Keep now-cast separate from election-day forecast, and archive original probabilities, expected margins, and predictive ranges.
- Before results, pick illustrative states using forecast-time evidence and say why each is informative. Show the forecast’s expected margin and interval beside the observed margin; report signed and absolute error. Mark late, provisional, and final results distinctly.
- Make uncertainty concrete: for a binary win outcome, probability describes repeated comparable elections; for a margin, show a distribution/range. An even win chance can describe either a narrow contest or wide uncertainty about a decisive win. Never infer “nail-biter” from 50/50 alone.
- Prestate what would challenge the model: named state-margin errors, observed outcomes outside forecast intervals, coordinated misses beyond modeled joint uncertainty, and systematic probability-score weakness against an always-50/50 binary baseline and stronger relevant baseline. Baselines and competitive subsets must be fixed before outcomes. A one-election score is a diagnostic, not a calibration verdict.
- Explain both directions of accountability: broad intervals cannot get a high uncertainty grade merely for covering the result; a sharp forecast should pay when it misses, with proper distribution/probability scores and coverage plus width. Do not assign letter-grade thresholds or aggregate grades without a reviewed rubric.

## Unresolved for critique round

1. Which exact archived preferred-model forecast version and timestamp will be graded, and which official source/status defines final state margins?
2. Which proper margin-distribution score, probability score, interval coverage/width display, baseline, and dependence-aware cohort are understandable and defensible for one election night?
3. Can the model provide joint draws or shared-error components so the guide can set a pre-outcome criterion for a cluster of correlated misses?
4. What public phrasing preserves the distinction between “70% chance,” the expected margin, and the chance of a close result without creating false confidence?
5. How should the guide acknowledge that the forecast may have been probabilistically reasonable but communicated as certainty, while still permitting a meaningful mechanism and accuracy critique?
