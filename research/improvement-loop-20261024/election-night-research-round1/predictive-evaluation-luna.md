# Round 1 — Evaluating an election-night scorecard

## Recommendation

Freeze and publish the preferred forecast, timestamp, horizon, contest universe, outcome definitions, point-summary rule, interval levels, baselines, and scoring/aggregation rules before results arrive. Keep a readable scoreboard beside an uncertainty panel. Make no letter grade until owners choose and defend thresholds from historical, out-of-sample election-cycle evidence. A single election can score this forecast; it cannot establish general calibration or identify a universal model quality.

## What to show and score

For each contest show: frozen expected margin and its exact meaning (for example, candidate A minus B in vote-share points); result margin from a named final source; signed and absolute point error; forecast and result timestamps/status; winner probability; selected predictive intervals; and whether the contest is called, final, unresolved, or rule-dependent. A point summary must be fixed in advance (mean or median, not whichever looks better after the fact). A provisional call is not a final margin. Count calls only on the frozen common cohort, with unresolved contests separate.

Use different targets and scores for different claims:

* **Point margin:** report signed bias and absolute error (and, descriptively, RMSE). State aggregation explicitly: equal contest weighting answers a different question from electorate-weighting. A tiny signed average can hide large offsetting misses, so pair signed bias with absolute errors.
* **Winner probability:** score each binary race with Brier score and/or log score; lower is better. Compare on the same races/horizon against a 50/50 forecast and a predeclared simple reference (for example, a historical/base-rate or poll-only rule). A model that always says 50/50 may tie the 50/50 baseline, but has shown no skill over it and should not receive a top predictive grade. For a multi-candidate winner target, score the full categorical distribution (multiclass Brier or ranked probability score), not a binary 50/50 straw baseline. Proper scores reward truthful probabilities in expectation; raw winner hit rate does not distinguish 51% from 99%. [Gneiting & Raftery, *Strictly Proper Scoring Rules*](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437); [Murphy, Brier score decomposition](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0493%281973%29012%3C0595%3AANVPOT%3E2.0.CO%3B2).
* **Full margin distribution:** use CRPS on the signed margin distribution, a proper score that evaluates the whole CDF; add interval score at preregistered levels, which penalizes both misses and width. Show empirical coverage and average width side by side. CRPS/interval score prevents a forecast from winning simply by making intervals arbitrarily broad; coverage alone rewards that evasion. Sharpness means concentration, but should be prized only subject to calibration. [Gneiting & Raftery (2007)](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jrssb.pdf); [Gneiting, Balabdaoui & Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1111/J.1467-9868.2007.00587.X).
* **Baseline skill:** publish score differences or skill relative to each declared reference, with paired comparisons on identical contests. 50/50 is a useful binary ignorance baseline, but it is not the sole “strong” comparator; use simple rules that could plausibly be deployed with the same information/horizon. Reference choice changes the claim, so show the actual baseline and raw scores rather than a standalone skill number. [Murphy & Daan, scoring rules](https://doi.org/10.1175/2009MWR2945.1).

A 50/50 win chance says only that either side is equally likely to win. It does not imply a close margin: broad distribution centered near zero can still place substantial mass on large wins. Show expected/median margin, a margin interval, and preregistered probabilities of “close” versus “decisive” outcomes. Define those margin bands before seeing results. Do not use a close/decisive label as a substitute for the distribution.

## Dependence and finite cycles

States and districts share national and regional shocks; treating their results as independent replications overstates evidence. Use saved joint simulations to show expected number of point errors beyond preregistered tolerances, chamber seat distributions, and coordinated miss patterns (for example, many same-direction state errors). A shared national miss can challenge the common-shock/measurement assumptions; a localized miss may instead implicate state-specific inputs. Neither alone proves a mechanism. For joint-distribution evaluation, energy score is available for ensemble draws, but it can be insensitive to dependence errors; supplement with a dependence-targeting variogram score or preregistered diagnostics. If joint predictive draws are unavailable or unsuitable, label dependence assessment qualitative rather than infer it from independent marginal race scores. [Scheuerer & Hamill (2015)](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/22327/).

The many races in one election are not many independent national elections. Report contest-level scores as descriptive for this cycle, and assess recurring calibration/grade thresholds with rolling historical hindcasts across cycles, keeping an election-cycle unit of uncertainty (not a naive race-level confidence interval). Repeated daily forecast snapshots are also not independent cases. Election forecasting literature cautions that cross-state correlation sharply limits what many states reveal about reliability; some authors argue the data are too sparse to establish presidential calibration at all. That is a real counterweight to a confident A–F label. [Grimmer, Knox & Westwood, *Assessing the Reliability of Probabilistic U.S. Presidential Election Forecasts*](https://dcknox.github.io/files/GrimmerKnoxWestwood_AssessingReliabilityPresidentialElectionForecasts.pdf).

## 2016: useful failure case, not a verdict

FiveThirtyEight’s contemporaneous final update gave Clinton a 72% win chance (Trump about 28%), while its popular-vote forecast favored Clinton by 3.6 points. Trump’s win was therefore a surprising but explicitly allowed outcome—not proof by itself that 72% was miscalibrated. The outcome also does not excuse a badly specified model if repeated comparable forecasts score poorly. The contemporaneous article explained the chance through close state margins and ordinary polling-error risk. [Final update, Nov. 8 2016](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/final-election-update-theres-a-wide-range-of-outcomes-and-most-of-them-come-up-clinton/).

Counterevidence matters: the National Council on Public Polls’ post-election report found an overall polling miss with several contributors, including late movement and education weighting; it did not support the popular “shy Trump voter” explanation as the principal cause. Poll error, forecast probability, media framing, and the winner call are distinct objects. [AAPOR task force report](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfx047). A useful scorecard must predeclare explanatory predictions and measurable diagnostics; it cannot infer a model mechanism from the winner alone. Headlines that collapse “favored” into “certain” are a communication failure even if the underlying probability is calibrated. The scorecard itself should say “more likely” and visualize the remaining probability.

## Bottom A–F rubric: research gate, not grades for this cycle

Use four visible dimensions: (1) point-margin accuracy/bias; (2) probability and distribution scores versus references; (3) interval coverage **with width/proper-score penalty**; (4) dependence/chamber performance, where joint artifacts exist. Keep the dimensions separate; any overall letter needs a published aggregation and missing-data rule. A conceptual scale can describe A as strong evidence of useful skill and calibrated precision, B as clear but qualified skill, C as mixed/near-reference performance, D as weak performance with meaningful misses, and F as severe, repeated failure against preregistered criteria. These are not numeric thresholds and must not be assigned ad hoc. Derive cutoffs from pre-election historical hindcasts and show score uncertainty; do not let excellent coverage alone earn A, nor one low-probability result force F. If cycle-level evidence cannot distinguish grades, report “insufficient evidence” alongside the dimensions.

## Unresolved owner decisions / critique targets

1. Which frozen horizons exist, and are forecasts comparable at one common cutoff? Which authoritative source defines final margins, recounts, runoffs, and candidate replacements?
2. Which simple baselines can be reconstructed without hindsight for every included contest and horizon? Define competitive subset by forecast-time evidence and freeze it.
3. What point target/summary, margin-band cutoffs, contest weighting, and chamber score can be generated from retained artifacts?
4. Do joint draws exist for the standard model and alternatives, and do they preserve shared shocks? Which variogram/dependence diagnostics are stable with a single election?
5. What election-cycle hindcast set is genuinely out-of-sample, and how should changing contest mixes be handled? Can any public A–F bands be justified with few cycles?
6. Which model explanations make observable, pre-election predictions? Name a failure pattern and a plausible alternative explanation before seeing outcomes.
