# Election-night scorecard: public communication research, round 1

**Purpose.** A watcher’s guide to one frozen, preferred forecast: what it predicted, what happened, how much uncertainty it assigned, and what evidence would count against its claims. The sources below are primary theory or empirical studies. Most communication experiments concern weather, time series, or inflation; applying them to elections is a design inference that should be tested with election users.

## Communication findings and design implications

**Define what the probability refers to.** Gigerenzer et al.’s survey across four countries found that interpretations of “30% chance of rain” varied; only in New York did a majority give the authors’ intended meteorological interpretation. They recommend specifying a reference class ([primary study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1539-6924.2005.00608.x)). But a later U.S. public survey found numerical percentages were often preferred to relative-frequency or odds formats, and preference did not guarantee comprehension ([Morss et al. 2008](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/wefo/23/5/2008waf2007088_1.xml); see also the experiment summarized in the [Met Office Weather Game paper](https://gc.copernicus.org/articles/2/101/2019/)). **Inference:** retain the exact numeric probability, name the event and reference class in plain language, and test a short explanation against the number alone. Avoid implying one election is a draw from 100 independent identical elections.

**Separate chance of winning from margin and close-race likelihood.** A 50% win probability alone says only that the forecast puts equal probability on the two sides of the win threshold. A narrow distribution centered near zero and a broad distribution spanning landslide outcomes can both produce 50%. Gelman, Hullman, and Wlezien explicitly distinguish vote share from win probability: a 60% win chance can correspond to an essentially tied expected election, while 60% vote share is a landslide ([2020 election-forecast communication paper](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/published/jdm200907b.pdf)). This is a conceptual example, not a claim that every model’s 60% forecast has the same margin distribution.

**Inference:** present three separate quantities when available: (1) win probability, (2) signed expected/median margin plus a clearly labeled predictive interval, and (3) probability of falling within predeclared close-margin bands. State the definition of “close” beside the number. Use separate graphic channels (for example, a probability bar for win chance and a horizontal margin interval centered on zero), with identical candidate/group and vote-share denominators in forecast and result. Do not make the whole interval look like a narrow race just because the win bar is near 50%. If the native result is a conditional principal-pair share, label it conditional and do not present it as a full-population vote forecast.

**Use uncertainty graphics sparingly and explain them.** The Met Office Weather Game was a large online experiment (8,220 completers) comparing forecast displays. Its temperature results varied by scenario: added percentile information helped some tasks but confused others; a more detailed fan display was not consistently best. The authors note self-selection, a game rather than real stakes, and a limited set of scenarios ([Stephens et al. 2019](https://gc.copernicus.org/articles/2/101/2019/)). In a separate online experiment (N=239), participants using 95% prediction intervals performed better than point-only advice, while ensemble plots with more information did not outperform intervals; the authors report a non-linear relationship between uncertainty display and performance ([Leffrang et al. 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/for.3222)). Neither experiment establishes a best election graphic. **Inference:** prefer one clearly labeled interval with coverage and a concise explanation over an ensemble of simulation traces by default; expose more detail on demand. Label prediction intervals as model-assigned coverage, not plausible bounds or guarantees.

**Show dependence and shared misses.** Election outcomes across states are not independent replications of national error. Election forecasting research warns that cross-state error correlation matters to uncertainty in national/chamber outcomes ([Caughey and Warshaw, 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207015000102); [Gelman, Hullman, and Wlezien, 2020](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/published/jdm200907b.pdf)). A shared national miss can move many states together, so 20 same-direction state errors do not equal 20 independent tests of the national error model. **Inference:** show state/race errors together, summarize shared direction/size and regional deviations, and compare chamber outcomes using joint draws when available. State that one election supplies few independent shocks; don’t use race count as the sample size for universal calibration claims.

## Accountability and a defensible scorecard

**Keep distinct forecast targets and failure modes.** Freeze the forecast timestamp, horizon, candidate/group definitions, scoring cohort, point-prediction rule, result source, and result status before results arrive. The first panel can list predicted margin/probability, observed result, signed and absolute error, and whether the result remains unresolved. Give provisional, called, and certified status with source and timestamp; don’t score an unfinished count as a final margin. Select illustrative races using forecast-time criteria and explain the selection rule. This guards against post-result cherry-picking; it is a design recommendation, not a finding from a user experiment.

**Score probabilities properly, but don’t call one score a full diagnosis.** A strictly proper scoring rule makes the truthful probability forecast optimal in expectation; Brier/log scores are appropriate for binary outcomes and CRPS can evaluate full distributions ([Gneiting & Raftery 2007](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/people/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)). Their calibration–sharpness framework says calibration is forecast-and-outcome consistency, while sharpness is concentration of forecasts; sharpness is desirable only subject to calibration ([Gneiting, Balabdaoui & Raftery 2007](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/J.1467-9868.2007.00587.X)). Thus report proper score against a same-target baseline, calibration diagnostics over many genuinely comparable forecasts, and interval coverage together with width. A broad forecast may cover the result but be uninformative; a narrow forecast may be sharp but miscalibrated. One election can reveal a bad realized score or conspicuous miss, but cannot establish long-run calibration by itself.

**Use matching baselines and predeclared cohorts.** Always-50/50 is a useful binary-outcome reference, not a margin or categorical House baseline. For categorical outcomes, use an explicit base-rate/structural forecast on the same outcome space. Compare both models on the same contests, horizon, and result rules; state whether any competitive subset was selected before outcomes. Report skill relative to the baseline instead of grading raw accuracy alone. Never use a binary score for conditional shares or unresolved multi-party/group outcomes.

**Do not invent letter cutoffs.** A–F should be a compact summary of a public, owner-reviewed rubric, not a pseudo-scientific threshold guessed after seeing results. A defensible rubric must disclose the aggregation and weights, use proper scores and baseline skill, constrain grades with coverage plus interval width, and report point-error performance separately from probabilistic performance. Publish raw components beside any letter. State before the election what patterns would challenge: confident errors, systematic directional misses, poor calibration across an appropriate archive, poor scores against the baseline, undercovered intervals, or a joint pattern incompatible with claimed shared uncertainty. Separately identify a surprising realized outcome, an inaccurate point estimate, systematic probability failure, a communication failure, and evidence against a proposed mechanism. A nonzero assigned probability is not a post-hoc excuse for poor aggregate performance, but a single low-probability event is not proof the probability was wrong.

## Counterevidence, failure scenarios, open questions

- Numeric probabilities may be preferred yet misread; a visual layer may help one task and hurt another. The reported experiments are mostly weather, simulated decision tasks, time-series advice, or inflation; election users, political stakes, and map/ribbon conventions may behave differently. Test comprehension of win chance versus margin directly.
- Fan/ensemble graphics can overload users or invite treating interval boundaries as hard limits. Color-map encoding may also imply binary certainty. Any graphic needs a key, text alternative, and mobile check.
- Calibration bins and letter grades are unstable with small, dependent election samples. One election cannot support an A–F claim of universal model quality. What archive and pooling scheme are appropriate for this model and target?
- What exact frozen horizon and result source/status apply to each Senate/House quantity? Which predictive draws support margin bands and dependence-aware chamber summaries? How are same-party races, Other groups, runoffs, and unresolved outcomes scored?
- Which point forecast is used for the scoreboard (mean, median, mode, or another declared statistic), and how should its score relate to the distribution score? What independent historical archive, cohort and baseline will calibrate a letter rubric before any 2026 outcome is known?

**Round-1 recommendation:** design the page around an immutable, sourced forecast/results table plus three distinct uncertainty views (win chance, margin distribution, close-margin probability), a concise shared-error/chamber panel, and raw evaluation components. Leave A–F blank or explicitly provisional until criteria, baselines, data archive, and aggregation have been reviewed before the election.
