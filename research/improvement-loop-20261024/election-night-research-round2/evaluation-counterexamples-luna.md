# Round 2 — Counterexamples for the election-night evaluation proposal

**Status:** research proposals for root/model-owner review, not approved rubric or implementation guidance. Read alongside the three Round 1 memos on predictive evaluation, public communication, and 2016 accountability.

## Corrections to Round 1

1. The first memo suggested “multiclass Brier or ranked probability score” for multi-candidate winner forecasts. That was too loose. A ranked probability score (RPS) rewards probability near the realized category **in a specified order**; the classic RPS was introduced for ranked categories. Democratic, Republican, and Other winner labels have no inherent rank. Unless the outcome really is ordinal and its order is substantively justified in advance, use a multicategory Brier score or logarithmic score on the full, exhaustive probability vector. Do not order D/R/O alphabetically or by current vote share. [Epstein, “A Scoring System for Probability Forecasts of Ranked Categories”](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450%281969%29008%3C0985%3AASSFPF%3E2.0.CO%3B2); [Gneiting & Raftery (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437).
2. The first memo wrongly called the 2016 review a “National Council on Public Polls” report. It is the [American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) task-force report, *An Evaluation of 2016 Election Polls in the U.S.*](https://aapor.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/AAPOR-2016-Election-Polling-Report.pdf). Attribute findings to that task force. AAPOR reports state-poll error and identifies late preference change and education weighting among explanations with evidence; it treats explanations cautiously. Do not turn that into a claim that one polling flaw explains every forecast miss. [AAPOR’s own report listing and synopsis](https://aapor.org/polling-accuracy/).

## Scorecard stress tests

**Always 50/50 vs a useful base rate vs a confident favorite.** For one binary race, Brier loss is `(forecast probability − outcome)^2` (lower is better). If candidate A wins, forecasts of 50%, 70%, and 95% produce losses .25, .09, and .0025: this single result rewards the overconfident favorite most. If A loses, losses are .25, .49, and .9025: the same confident forecast is punished heavily. Assuming a genuinely comparable archive supports a 70% base rate, expected losses over many such cases are .25 for 50/50, .21 for 70%, and .2725 for 95%. Thus a lucky win is not evidence that overconfidence was sound, and a single loss is not proof of miscalibration. Proper scoring rules have their incentive property **in expectation**, not as a promise about a one-election ranking. [Brier (1950)](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0493%281950%29078%3C0001%3AVOFEIT%3E2.0.CO%3B2); [Gneiting & Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437).

The baseline also matters. A flat 50/50 forecast cannot demonstrate information beyond ignorance; a defensible base-rate/reference forecast can outperform it when contests in the frozen evaluation cohort are systematically imbalanced. Estimate that reference using only forecast-time information and comparable historical cases, never the outcomes being graded. Report both raw score and paired difference versus references on the same contests/horizon. Do not give 50/50 a special penalty for “not calling” a winner: that would reward decisiveness, not probability quality. Winner hit rate may be shown as a simple count but cannot stand in for probability score.

**Same win chance, different margin uncertainty.** Two candidate-A margin forecasts can both assign exactly 50% to A winning and have median margin zero, while one concentrates tightly near zero and the other spreads substantial probability across comfortable wins for either side. Their binary Brier outcomes are identical for a given winner, and winner hit rate is identical; the full margin CRPS and interval scores distinguish them. The guide therefore needs both target spaces. “50/50” alone cannot be scored as “tight race.” State the margin sign convention and show expected/median margin, interval, and predeclared close/decisive probabilities.

**100% interval coverage by vagueness.** A model can issue huge intervals that include every result and boast perfect observed coverage. Coverage alone does not reward useful precision. The interval score explicitly combines width and a penalty for observations outside the interval; CRPS scores the full predictive distribution. Show coverage and mean width, plus a proper distribution score. A huge interval may still be appropriate if the model assigned that uncertainty, but should not receive high marks on uncertainty quality merely for containing the outcome. [Gneiting & Raftery’s interval-score treatment](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf).

**Many coordinated misses are not many independent tests.** Suppose numerous states miss toward the same party. Under independent marginal race forecasts, multiplying their tail probabilities would make the cluster appear fantastically unlikely. A joint model with national/regional shocks may assign the coordinated pattern appreciable probability. Report the joint simulated seat/count distribution and compare the realized vector using supported multivariate diagnostics; variogram scores can be more sensitive to dependence misspecification than energy score, though neither creates multiple election cycles from one vector. If the retained standard-model artifact lacks joint draws, this claim cannot be scored from separate state probabilities: present the shared-miss pattern descriptively and flag dependence evaluation as unavailable. A cluster can challenge the predicted shared-error structure if it fell in a preregistered low-probability region, but one observed cluster cannot prove which mechanism failed. [Scheuerer & Hamill (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1175/MWR-D-14-00269.1); [Grimmer, Knox & Westwood](https://osf.io/6g5zq).

**Partial counts and D/R/O outcomes.** Do not compare a frozen pre-election margin against a changing partial-count lead as if both were final outcomes. Display each return’s source, timestamp, counted/estimated status, and race-call status; mark score as pending. A model conditioned on partial returns is a different, updated forecast with a new time horizon and must not replace the frozen pre-election forecast. Score the pre-election margin only under an explicit final-result rule/source, and keep a called-but-not-certified margin visibly provisional if that distinction matters to the source. For a D/R/O target, define D, R, and Other as exhaustive, mutually exclusive classes before results. Preserve the Other category in the result and forecast denominator; do not silently renormalize D/R shares to 100%, relabel a third-party winner, or treat “Other” as ordered for RPS. If the model forecasts candidate identities, grouping those candidates into party categories is a new aggregation and must be fixed for both forecast and result.

## Which claims are actually falsifiable?

A named point estimate plus fixed metric, target, and final result permits an exact error statement in this election. A probability forecast permits an exact realized proper-score contribution. An interval permits a covered/not-covered observation and an interval score. A joint forecast can assign the realized seat vector a joint score, if the saved artifact supports it. These are direct, auditable observations—not yet a universal judgment of model quality.

Pre-register *evidence against* stronger claims: a point-error pattern inconsistent with a named precision claim; poorer paired proper scores than declared baselines across frozen cohorts; persistent reliability deviations on a genuinely comparable archive; undercoverage or excessive width relative to the model’s stated intervals; or joint patterns in a preregistered tail that recur across cycles. Calibration is a property of forecast/outcome collections, not something one race can confirm. A single outside-interval result is evidence against that forecast instance, not proof the model is unusable; a nonzero probability does not immunize a model from repeated bad scores. A mechanism claim is testable only if its observable implications were specified before results (for example, magnitude of shared national error), and a mismatch does not uniquely identify the cause.

For public A–F grading, a conceptual A/B/C/D/F description alone is not reproducible. Even if component cutoffs are anchored to hindcasts, choices remain about contest weighting, election-cycle weighting, baseline, multiple horizons, incomplete races, aggregation weights, and evidence uncertainty. Tuning cutoffs and evaluating them on the same small historical cycle archive risks overfitting; reserve cycles for evaluation or label the rubric exploratory. A single 2026 election can support a **cycle-specific scoreboard**, but cannot fairly support an A for long-run calibration or an F for every probabilistic claim. The presidential-elections reliability study argues that state results add limited leverage because within-election errors are correlated and election cycles are rare. Counterpoint: this does not make a frozen forecast immune from scrutiny—its point errors, realized scores, interval behavior, and observable mechanisms remain reportable. Use “insufficient evidence” rather than a confident letter where the historical record cannot distinguish grades.

## Review questions before any public rubric

- Which target matters for each score: margin, winner, full D/R/O category, or chamber seat count? Are the classes and “Other” definition frozen?
- What named final-result source, certification/call rule, and treatment of recounts/runoffs governs scoring? Which partial result states remain pending?
- What genuine comparable historical archive supports each baseline and grade calibration, and are cycle-level differences reported rather than treating all races/daily snapshots as independent?
- Are joint simulations retained for the default stack and do they include national/regional shared shocks? What dependence score can be interpreted at the available dimension and sample size?
- What decision rule keeps point accuracy, probability/distribution quality, intervals, and dependence visible without arbitrary or double-counted weighting? Who reviews any threshold before election day?
