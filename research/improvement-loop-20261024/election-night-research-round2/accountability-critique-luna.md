# Round 2: Public accountability critique

## Correct the 2016 record before using it as an example

“PEC gave Clinton 99%” is true for a specific earlier vintage, but is not the final election-day forecast. On Nov. 6, Sam Wang wrote that PEC’s win estimate had been in the 98–99% range; he also disclosed that this depended on a judgmental, aggressive home-stretch polling-error assumption, and wrote that a less aggressive alternative implied 91–93% (later revised to 95% with a 1.1-point assumption). He acknowledged difficulty estimating probabilities above 90%, explained that the site’s activism-oriented use case affected what he considered “basically settled,” and said he would not change the then-current calculation after receiving feedback. The page is unusually useful accountability evidence because it preserves both the forecast and a visible model-choice discussion. ([PEC, Nov. 6](https://election.princeton.edu/articles/all-estimates-point-toward-hrc50-probability-what-determines-the-exact-number/))

PEC’s Nov. 8 final projection reported a 93% Clinton win probability under a revised polling-error assumption of ±1.1%, not 99%. It separately reported the Electoral College mode (Clinton 323), median (307), one-sigma interval (281–326), and popular-vote estimate (Clinton +4.0 ±0.6). These numbers refer to different forecast objects. The Nov. 6 sensitivity note’s 95% with 1.1-point uncertainty is not the Nov. 8 result; the underlying poll snapshot/Meta-Margin had changed by then. PEC’s Nov. 6 method says the snapshot compounds state-poll medians/SEs across 56 contests, while the November forecast adds uncertainty about movement from that snapshot. The scorecard must archive the contemporaneous output and explanation, not use a secondary “99%” summary as canonical. ([PEC final projections](https://election.princeton.edu/articles/final-projections-2016/); [PEC Nov. 6 method discussion](https://election.princeton.edu/articles/all-estimates-point-toward-hrc50-probability-what-determines-the-exact-number/))

FiveThirtyEight’s final Nov. 8 post is also specific: 71% win probability in polls-only, 72% in polls-plus, and Clinton +3.6 points in its popular-vote forecast. They are two model stacks, not one interchangeable “538 number”; their probability refers to the Electoral College win and the +3.6 refers to popular-vote margin. The article emphasizes a wide range of possible EV outcomes. Preserve the named model and timestamp whenever quoting the win probability. ([FiveThirtyEight final update, indexed contemporaneous text](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/final-election-update-theres-a-wide-range-of-outcomes-and-most-of-them-come-up-clinton/))

**Implication:** a 99% earlier PEC estimate, 93% final PEC estimate, 71% FiveThirtyEight polls-only, and 72% polls-plus cannot be grouped as “the forecasts” or scored as though they shared a horizon. A forecast can change before election day; that change is itself part of the record. In any user-facing 2016 comparison, label the model, vintage, target, and point/range statistic together.

## How the scorecard could still excuse a bad forecast

“One election cannot prove calibration” is necessary but easy to misuse. It must not become “so no criticism is possible.” Even for one cycle, visitors can evaluate frozen point margins, probability and distribution scores over a declared race cohort, interval hits and misses, and baseline skill. What they cannot infer from that single cycle is long-run calibration or universal model quality. A proper probability score being a valid realized score is not a license to call it a reliable estimate of future quality.

Pre-outcome falsification criteria should identify observable ways the forecast could lose, without claiming that one observation deterministically falsifies a stochastic model. For each named, forecast-time-selected state, publish expected/median margin, defined margin denominator, predictive intervals at fixed levels, win probability, and probability of falling within declared “close” bands. Show the certified final margin against these quantities. An outside-interval observation is evidence against that state’s predictive distribution; several same-direction misses are more concerning if the model assigned low probability to their joint occurrence. The guide should say “evidence against,” not “proof false.” The possible close bands and state selection must be fixed before results.

The model’s joint uncertainty is decisive. If joint draws exist, compare the observed vector of selected state margins, regional shifts, national vote, and chamber outcome with the model’s joint distribution using a predeclared diagnostic. Show how often draws produce a similarly coordinated miss. Do not multiply marginal state probabilities, count each state as an independent national trial, or report a small “surprise” number unless the dependence model is represented. Marginal intervals alone cannot substantiate the probability of a multi-state miss. If the model does not retain joint draws or an equivalent shared-error artifact, mark the joint claim “not evaluable from saved forecast output.”

Point-error and probability findings need separate columns. A wrong median or mean margin is an assessable point-prediction miss even if the actual margin was within a broad interval. A correct winner call does not cure poor margin skill. Conversely, one interval miss does not by itself invalidate an interval intended to cover, say, 90% of comparable outcomes. Publish the raw per-race residuals, signed and absolute error, proper distribution score if available, interval coverage plus width, and relative score against matching references. That combination makes both confident error and evasive vagueness visible.

## Rubric, baselines, and public meaning

**A–F:** a letter is a summary of a rule, not a substitute for one. The three initial memos rightly reject invented cutoffs, but a purely qualitative A–F paragraph remains subjective and invites post-result reinterpretation. Owners should define dimensions separately (point accuracy, probability/distribution skill, coverage with width, dependence/joint behavior), a pre-election aggregation rule if a single letter is required, and a “not enough comparable evidence” state. A reasonable design hypothesis is to show a letter only for dimensions with reviewed, predeclared thresholds derived from comparable archived forecasts; otherwise publish component results and mark the letter “not graded.” Do not give a universal model-quality A/F based on a single presidential cycle. A user-comprehension study should test whether readers understand that a realized D/F cycle rating and a long-term model-quality claim are not identical.

**Baseline:** always-50/50 is a reference for binary win probabilities, not an all-purpose baseline. It is intentionally uninformative and may be weak compared with a simple information-matched poll average, historical/base-rate model, or prior-vote-plus-current-poll rule. The scorecard should name a small, defensible set in advance and score every reference on identical contests, horizon, target, and result rules. For a multi-candidate or grouped outcome, the baseline must assign probabilities on that same categorical outcome space. Show raw proper scores and paired skill differences; do not hide an unfavorable comparison behind an arbitrary baseline. Define the competitive cohort from forecast-time data and show an all-contest view as well, so “challenger” selection cannot be tailored after seeing the miss.

**50/50:** “an even chance of either winner” is more precise than “a toss-up” unless “close margin” is independently established. A narrow distribution centered at zero and a broad distribution with material probability on either candidate winning decisively can both yield 50% win probability. The visual should put a win-chance bar next to a separate signed-margin interval and a labeled probability of a fixed close-margin event. Ask viewers to interpret one narrow and one broad 50% example; whether a legend alone fixes the misconception is an open comprehension question.

## Result completeness and live use

Keep the frozen forecast visible while results arrive; do not silently substitute a live update for the election-day prediction. Pair each result with source, last-updated time, reporting/count status, and one of called, provisional, certified/final, unresolved, or rule-dependent. Early returns are partial samples: do not score their current margin against a forecast for the final margin, and do not imply a called winner supplies the final vote margin. A later certified result can update the observed column while preserving the old status/timestamp history. Report the declared cohort denominator and unresolved count; do not quietly drop a late or contested race from accuracy totals. Grade final margins only after the stated finality rule is met; show provisional scoreboard separately if useful.

## Simplest guide hypotheses to comprehension-test

1. **Forecast card:** model and cutoff; “what question this answers”; expected margin; fixed prediction interval; win probability; simple verbal frequency; link to methodology.
2. **Selected-state table:** why selected before results; forecast margin/probability/range; live result and completeness status; final signed/absolute error and whether the result fell in the interval.
3. **Shared-error card:** a single plain-language national/region/chamber summary from frozen joint draws, including the frequency of similarly coordinated misses; else say the joint risk cannot be scored from retained artifacts.
4. **Evaluation strip:** point, probability, and distribution performance kept separate; baseline and cohort named; letter dimensions/grades hidden or “not graded” until pre-reviewed criteria are approved.

User testing must include whether people distinguish 71% from a 71-point margin, 50/50 from a narrow margin, interval coverage from a guarantee, dependent state misses, and partial from final results.

### Questions for the next critique

1. Which joint diagnostic is sufficiently interpretable and statistically defensible for a small, correlated contest set, and which artifact can the preferred model actually export?
2. Can historical forecast vintages be reconstructed consistently enough to support thresholds for each public grade, without mixing model versions or target definitions?
3. Is a per-cycle letter helpful if probabilities are assessed across dependent races but calibration is a repeated-forecast property? Would multidimensional labels be clearer?
4. What exact denominator and status display best prevents a partly counted state from looking like a final point-prediction miss?
