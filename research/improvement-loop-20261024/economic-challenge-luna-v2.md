# E01 challenge: inflation-first fundamentals feature

October 25, 2026. This is a design challenge, not a model fit, ranking, or recommended adjustment to any current forecast. It uses the current House/Senate count-graph definitions and the economic vintage packets already reviewed in this lab. The central question is whether one inflation feature adds information to the existing choice mean, not whether inflation is unpopular or whether a party deserves a penalty.

## What the current graph already says

The selected count-graph `Fundamentals` role already has an intercept, relative lean, previous presidential-party carryover, a president-party-by-midterm term, and candidate incumbency. The midterm coefficient has a zero-centered prior; the larger model also has shared cycle uncertainty and race-level residual uncertainty. These terms already capture broad cycle movement. An economic predictor belongs as a change to the existing fundamentals mean, fitted with that graph, not as another independent national shock and not as a downstream post-poll correction.

This matters for identification: a national CPI value is one value per election cycle. Hundreds of races within that cycle do not supply hundreds of independent economic shocks. The held-out 2018/2020/2022/2024 checkpoints are only four cycle realizations, split between two midterms (A: 2018, 2022) and two presidential elections (B: 2020, 2024). A/B cycle scores are descriptive stress tests, not enough evidence to fit an unconstrained midterm interaction or claim stable incremental prediction. Use every earlier source-supported cycle for training, report the actual number of independent cycles in each fit, and do not pool presidential and midterm outcomes as though they shared one estimand.

## What the literature does and does not support

- Abramowitz's study of 1974, 1978 and 1982 midterms finds that presidential evaluations had different associations with House voting across elections; the effects were stronger in some high-salience contests and in races involving an incumbent from the president's party. This supports retaining party responsibility and midterm context. Three old elections do not establish an inflation coefficient or a present-day calibration rule. [Journal of Politics article](https://doi.org/10.2307/2131064)
- In a 2022 congressional survey, Baccini and Weymouth find that reported personal inflation burden is associated with lower Democratic support; their embedded experiment tests competing explanations of inflation and shifts support in different directions. The experiment identifies effects of attribution messages, not the causal effect of national CPI on actual ballots. Mutz and Mansfield's 2020–2022 respondent panel, by contrast, finds no evidence that worsening economic perceptions or inflation accounted for party switching in the 2022 congressional election. These results bound the inference: inflation can be salient and connected to stated choice, but the 2022 House vote-switching evidence is mixed and individual-level. [Baccini and Weymouth](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007123424000930), [Mutz and Mansfield](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2319512121)
- A 2024 US presidential-election survey experiment found that asking respondents to think about price increases lowered evaluations of the Biden–Harris administration, especially among Democrats and independents, but did not measure the treatment effect on actual votes. It also notes that inflation perceptions are partisan and that responsibility attribution can vary across executive and legislative elections. This supports a salience hypothesis, not a fixed election-model penalty. [British Journal of Political Science article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-political-science/article/inflation-and-incumbent-support-experimental-evidence-from-the-2024-us-presidential-election/A00393BF40613670170EBE1AF1D4CA87)
- The 2026 Riaño–Trebbi county-level study uses local price and income variation over 2021–2024. It reports that real-wage declines, rather than higher inflation, predicted Republican gains in congressional results in that episode, while inflation retained an association with presidential vote shares. This is the sharpest current warning against assuming CPI is the best congressional predictor. Its short, unusual four-year period and cross-county identification do not establish an out-of-cycle national forecasting feature. Treat it as a strong competing hypothesis and replication target, not a coefficient to import. [NBER Working Paper 35301](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35301), [published version DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmoneco.2026.103977)
- Kim and Yang find that average commute time conditions presidential accountability for gasoline-price increases. That is evidence for an exposure interaction in presidential contests; it does not show that national gasoline change adds to headline CPI in congressional midterms. [American Politics Research article](https://doi.org/10.1177/1532673X211043445)

## Which single feature should go first?

**For a narrow inflation-first test, the defensible first feature is the latest released U.S. CPI-U all-items 12-month percent change**, calculated from the BLS NSA national series and its dated release vintage. It has a clear definition, a longer consistent history than the archived gasoline panel, and directly tests the requested inflation hypothesis. BLS defines CPI as a measure of change over time; its index level is relative to a reference period and is not a cross-area cost-of-living scale. The 12-month rate measures price growth over that year, not the change in the inflation rate, the cumulative amount prices have risen, or a household's real-income loss. [BLS CPI concepts](https://www.bls.gov/opub/hom/cpi/concepts.htm)

So this is an operational first experiment, not a literature-backed claim that headline inflation is the uniquely best economic variable. For the congressional outcome target, a vintage-matched **real wage or real disposable-income growth** feature is a serious head-to-head alternative because the 2026 study separates real wage loss from inflation and reports a different congressional pattern. The real-income series needs its own release-vintage and measurement review before it can compete. [BEA definition of real disposable personal income](https://www.bea.gov/help/glossary/real-disposable-personal-income)

Do not start with an inflation bundle. Do not include all-items CPI, core CPI, headline-minus-core, gasoline, real income, and a hand-built “cost pressure” index together in F1: too many overlapping features for scarce independent cycles. Core CPI is a possible later replacement/sensitivity, not a causal purification of headline. The 12-month headline measure includes gasoline, so adding national gas later must pass an incremental test against that headline baseline.

### Price level versus inflation rate

Keep the first version to one predeclared quantity:

`pi12_t = 100 * (CPI-U_all-items-NSA_t / CPI-U_all-items-NSA_(t-12) - 1)`

Use the same historical BLS release vintage for both index observations. This is price growth over the past year; it is not acceleration (a change in the inflation rate). It is not a measure of how much the basket costs, nor does a lower current inflation rate mean the accumulated price increase has reversed. If the question is persistent purchasing-power burden, predeclare a *separate alternative*, such as the log CPI-level change over the fixed previous 24 months. Compare that one-feature alternative against the 12-month rate in the same baseline graph; do not put both in simultaneously at first. A fixed 24-month horizon is still only a proxy for burden and does not account for income, taxes, household-specific baskets or earlier price changes.

The NBER 2026 result makes a third, distinct rival worth measuring later: real wage or real disposable-income growth. It is not interchangeable with either CPI rate or cumulative CPI change. The current four-cycle A/B evaluation cannot choose among three highly correlated macro stories on its own; use broader earlier-cycle training with strict vintages and preserve the A/B cycles as development checks.

## Party orientation and one-feature implementation

The mechanism to test is responsibility for prices, not a universal relationship between CPI and a party label. Keep the existing president-party and midterm terms intact. Add one CPI-driven utility contrast for the current president's party, with party membership bound from the authoritative event record. Define it in the model's exact choice coordinate only after confirming how the chosen Senate/House outcome port encodes party-relative utility; do not multiply by a party sign twice or add the CPI term uniformly to every candidate. A positive inflation rate should not be hard-coded as a Democratic penalty: historical party responsibility alternates, attribution varies, and the experimental evidence shows partisan and salience conditions.

One prespecified candidate for evaluation is:

- Standardize `pi12` using training-cycle mean and SD only; freeze those values for the held-out cycle.
- Add a single coefficient on the candidate-group contrast aligned to the party holding the presidency at that election; keep the coefficient sign free.
- Use a zero-centered symmetric prior, initially `Normal(0, 0.10)` on logit utility per training SD of CPI. This is a conservative engineering proposal, not an estimate from political-economy literature. Before fitting, check prior-predictive changes against the current `choice_president_midterm ~ Normal(0, 0.25)` and existing probability ranges. Prespecify a small prior-scale sensitivity (for example SD 0.05 and 0.20); do not choose among them using the held-out election scores.
- Fit the entire affected graph jointly for both arms. Arm A is the exact current graph. Arm B adds only the CPI term. Keep election inputs, dates, rosters, targets, support masks, all other priors, polls (when included), and simulation budgets the same. Do not hold the other posterior banks fixed while changing the fundamentals term.
- Primary empirical test: use **rolling earlier-only validation**. At each target cycle, train only on cycles strictly earlier than that target, then score the target cycle. Report log score/Brier for group or candidate outcomes, plus uncertainty and calibration, by cycle. Report midterm and presidential results separately; the four 2018–2024 checkpoints are not a broad generalization sample. Leave-one-whole-cycle-out evaluation that trains on future cycles may be shown separately as a retrospective sensitivity, never as the primary historical forecast simulation.

Investigate gains and material regressions by cycle and event type. A gain concentrated in one cycle or one event type is a limitation to explain, not an automatic veto; adoption should weigh the overall evidence and the stated target. Revise priors or specifications only as versioned development work, and do not describe such revisions as outcome-blind when the development results have been examined. Defer the term if gains vanish under earlier-only evaluation, depend on post-cutoff revisions, or are outweighed by material regressions on the stated target. An effect whose posterior remains near zero or whose predictive value is indistinguishable from the existing cycle term is not a reason to keep extra complexity.

## Gasoline: next only if it adds beyond headline CPI

Do not place gasoline in the first CPI challenger. CPI already incorporates gasoline expenditure; the national pump series and headline CPI therefore share signal. A useful second-stage question is whether a prespecified recent national regular-gas price change contributes incremental information beyond `pi12`. The existing TWIP packet has dated pre-cutoff issue pairs and a 13-week contrast for the four A/B cycles, with older-cycle support, but still marks original posting logs unauthenticated. Keep its vintage/status separate from the later EIA weekly-update schedule proxy and do not treat every reference date as proof of release.

If a commute-exposure interaction is later tested, it should use a pre-cutoff, fixed ACS commuting/vehicle exposure available before the election, centered and standardized on training data. It must be compared with existing state-economic and geographic/type terms. No state gas-price feature is available from the national series, and multiplying one national shock by hundreds of races does not create hundreds of independent price shocks. The Kim–Yang presidential result motivates this as a later hypothesis only.

## Data/vintage and overlap gates before any fit

1. **Bind the exact model cutoff first.** The CPI vintage packet uses 23:59:59 UTC on H14 calendar day. Sol reports that the count-graph historical input contract uses a `00:00 UTC` feature cutoff. These are not the same rule. Select source values against the actual owner contract, including exact-time release cases; do not join the packet wholesale until the cutoff is reconciled.
2. **Reconstruct monthly values from dated release bytes.** The packet preserves BLS release copies. It reports a concrete leakage issue in the legacy table: 2018's `cpi_yoy_oct=2.5` matches October CPI released after the election; 2022's value `8.2` matches September CPI and was released before the election. There are no 2020 or 2024 rows in `midterm_history.csv`. Do not reuse the October-named column without row-level vintage binding.
3. **Keep national and local variation distinct.** One national CPI realization moves across cycles only. State rows that repeat it do not identify a state-specific effect. A local-income/prices mechanism needs true dated local coverage and the common cycle structure, not a synthetic local CPI expansion.
4. **Respect target and horizon.** Use the same candidate/group count target as the selected count graph, not survey intentions, presidential vote alone, or the legacy House share-change target. Historical CPI release date and target election cutoff must be paired; release month is not availability date.
5. **Do not use gas to “explain” headline without a matching design.** `Gasoline × commute exposure` is one later interaction challenger. `Headline CPI − gasoline CPI` is not automatically a gasoline contribution, and a national retail gas change cannot stand in for a state household basket.

## Decision

Keep the current graph as the exact control. The authorized inflation-first run should test only the one-feature, party-oriented headline 12-month CPI term against it, with symmetric shrinkage and full joint refits. Label this as a narrow test of recent national price growth. Run real purchasing-power growth as a separate serious alternative after dated series admission. Add gasoline only as a later incremental challenger, not as a second national shock. The remaining scientific gates are exact cutoff reconciliation and dated source admission; the evidence does not establish the sign, predictive value, or 2026 electoral effect of any proposed term.
