# Gas prices, inflation, and U.S. midterms: bounded research note

**Date:** 2026-10-24. **Scope:** literature and source audit only. No fits, current-outcome prediction, or causal claim about the 2026 election.

### Release-cutoff problem in legacy CPI field

The legacy field `cpi_yoy_oct` is October’s CPI measured against the preceding October. BLS publishes that observation only after the month ends. In all recent midterms checked, it arrived after voting: October 2018 CPI was released Nov. 14 after the Nov. 6 election; October 2020 CPI was released Nov. 12 after Nov. 3; October 2022 CPI was released Nov. 10 after Nov. 8; and the 2024 BLS calendar scheduled October CPI for Nov. 13 after Nov. 5. See [BLS 2018 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_11142018.htm), [BLS 2020 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_11122020.htm), [BLS 2022 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_11102022.htm), and [BLS 2024 release calendar](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/2024/home.htm). For a pre-election hindcast, use the latest CPI release actually available by a fixed cutoff, likely September CPI (e.g. released Oct. 11 in 2018, Oct. 13 in 2022, Oct. 10 in 2024), not final October CPI. Reconstruct real-time vintages and release timestamps, rather than merely renaming the current October column.

The repo’s `snapshot_2026.json` was last updated May 17, 2026 and contains approval through Apr. 23, GDP through Q1, unemployment through April, and CPI through April; it is not a current October election-cutoff snapshot. As of this note’s Oct. 24, 2026 cutoff, BLS has released September CPI on Oct. 14 and scheduled October CPI for Nov. 10; EIA’s weekly regular-gasoline table runs through Oct. 19 (released Oct. 20). These official release records demonstrate what is available at this date, not whether the level is high/low, who benefits, or an election effect. Sources: [BLS 2026 release calendar](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/2026/), [BLS September 2026 CPI release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_10142026.htm), [EIA weekly retail gasoline series](https://www.eia.gov/dnav/pet/PET_PRI_GND_A_EPMR_PTE_DPGAL_W.htm).

## What the evidence supports—and what it does not

1. **Gas-price exposure (US presidential elections, observational):** Kim & Yang (2022) use average commuting time as exposure and find constituencies with longer driving times more likely to hold the president accountable for gasoline price increases. This supports testing *price change × pre-existing commuting exposure* as a pocketbook mechanism, but their target is presidential voting, not Senate/House midterm results, and the design is not a causal experiment. It does not justify a standalone gasoline-price penalty or transferring its coefficient to congressional races. [American Politics Research article](https://doi.org/10.1177/1532673X211043445).
2. **Inflation in the 2022 congressional cycle (survey/experiment):** Baccini & Weymouth’s pre-registered late-October survey of about 2,000 U.S. adults found higher reported personal inflation burden associated with lower Democratic support. Randomized attribution messages moved stated preferences: blaming government spending weakened Democratic support; a corporate-greed frame reduced Republicans’ perceived advantage on inflation management, with less direct movement in vote intention. This is useful evidence that attribution/framing matters, but the outcome is survey-reported preference and treatment effects, not realized district vote changes or a general cross-cycle forecast law. [British Journal of Political Science article](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007123424000930).
3. **Counterevidence on vote switching in 2022:** Mutz and Mansfield use a 2020–22 individual panel and find no evidence that changing inflation/economic perceptions predicted switching congressional vote toward Republicans; change in Biden evaluations did predict switching. Only a minority switched, so power is limited, and the estimand is individual vote switching among panel respondents, not aggregate House share/seat swing. The contrast with Baccini & Weymouth is informative: cross-sectional associations and stated survey intentions do not guarantee within-person switching or aggregate seat loss. [PNAS article](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2319512121). Root corrected the initial author attribution against the publication.
4. **Midterm performance is not a simple economic referendum:** Abramowitz (1985), using three midterm ANES/CPS elections (1974, 1978, 1982), found presidential evaluations varied in effect with national-issue salience and race/incumbency context. Bafumi, Erikson & Wlezien (2010) find generic House preferences tend to move toward the out-party as the midterm campaign progresses, interpreting the pattern as balancing rather than a consistent decline in presidential popularity or rise in presidential-performance salience. These findings support explicit midterm context and conditional effects, not a presumption that inflation or gas is the dominant driver. The current selected stack already has a president/midterm term, so an added economic feature must prove information beyond it. [Abramowitz](https://doi.org/10.2307/2131064); [Bafumi et al.](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022381610000113).

**Distinguish four objects:** inflation rate is the *pace of price increases*; price level/cumulative cost is how expensive the basket remains; real-income growth combines price and income change; gasoline is a volatile, visible component with geography and exposure. Gas-price increases and CPI overlap mechanically because motor fuel is in CPI. Do not put CPI, gasoline inflation, nominal gas prices, and real income into one unconstrained feature bundle and interpret each as an independent cause. Political blame, issue salience, survey intention, switching, vote share, seat change, and candidate win probability are different outcomes. Cross-country or presidential-election findings are not direct midterm congressional evidence.

## Smallest falsifiable challenger

If pursuing one new mechanism in the selected `Fundamentals` role, prioritize a **single national pre-cutoff CPI inflation measure** first because a consistent long historical series exists and the legacy model provides precedent, while treating the leakage repair as prerequisite. Then only if source coverage and sample support allow, test one separate gas-exposure contrast.

**Prespecified gas challenger (`midterm_gas_exposure_v1`, research design only):** for a fixed 7-day-before-Election-Day cutoff, use EIA national weekly regular-gas prices available by the cutoff and compute the 8-week change in gas prices. Interact that single national gas-price change with each state’s strictly earlier commuting exposure (car-commute share or mean commute time from a pinned ACS table/vintage published before the cutoff). If CPI is included, keep it as a **separate predictor** with its own explicitly stated 12-month inflation horizon; do not subtract a 12-month CPI change from an 8-week gas change and call the result a matched-horizon relative-price measure. A valid relative-price contrast would require gas and the broader price index measured over the same dates, with a clear scale/deflator definition. The ACS asks means of transportation and travel time to work; 5-year estimates are typically published in December, so only the latest actually released vintage is eligible ([Census ACS release/methodology](https://www.census.gov/topics/employment/commuting.html)). Center exposure nationally and shrink the single interaction strongly. Estimate sign freely; do not assert it must hurt the president’s party. This national shock gives states different exposure but does not create independent state-level gas prices; the effective shock sample is cycles, not state-races. EIA weekly national data are widely available; state/city retail series are limited to selected geographies, so do not fill missing states by pretending a PADD average is state-specific. Gasoline coefficients must allow price decreases to have a potentially different effect from increases only in a later prespecified asymmetry test; do not select a nonlinear hinge after inspecting election outcomes.

**Primary target and fair A/B:** bind the target to the model being challenged and keep it identical between arms: for the legacy macro model, use its House Democratic share-change target; for the selected Senate count graph, use the same candidate/count target and race/cycle splits as its current baseline. Do not score a House-share challenger against Senate candidate targets or reuse House coefficients as Senate parameters. Keep cycles, training splits, default prior features, poll inputs (if any), priors, and full joint refit identical within each pair. Compare (A) existing selected fundamentals + president/midterm context and (B) the same model plus the single prespecified gas-exposure term. Evaluate leave-one-election-cycle-out predictive log score / squared error and uncertainty, with cycle as the independent unit. Report the legacy all-four-economics model only as a separate reference; its 13 observations are not independent race-level evidence. If using only 2018 and 2022 current-quality source coverage, call the test descriptive/pilot: two midterm cycles cannot establish stable predictive value. Expand historical coverage only from verified historical releases/vintages.

**Falsification / stop rules:** reject or defer if a fixed cutoff cannot be reconstructed; if the exposure measure is revised/published after the target election; if benefit vanishes under each-cycle deletion or is driven solely by 2022; if predictive scores fail to improve under same-fit evaluation; if gas term is collinear with CPI, regional effects, presidential approval or poll signal and does not add held-out information; or if its sign/result is unstable across sensible frozen cutoffs and prior scales. Do not select based on an in-sample coefficient, 2026 mood/current prices, polling issue-salience headlines, or post-election fit.

## Data ownership and 2026 boundary

- **Inflation:** BLS CPI archived release vintages; latest observation must have a release timestamp before the forecast cutoff. Prefer all-items YoY plus a separate sensitivity to CPI excluding energy only if declared in advance.
- **Gasoline:** EIA weekly national regular retail gasoline series; preserve publication date, not only reference week. The primary interaction is national gas-price change × state commute exposure. EIA publishes retail series only for selected states/cities; national/PADD prices cannot be presented as state-specific.
- **Exposure:** historical ACS commuting data with the specific estimate vintage and publication date pinned; use a value known before each election. A post-election five-year ACS estimate is not allowed merely because it describes pre-election years.
- **Outcome/context:** House two-party vote-share swing/seat result and midterm status/party responsibility must be consistently coded. The current legacy CSV’s 13 midterm cycles through 2022 uses national October CPI and contains no 2024 row. Its October CPI values must not be scored as pre-election predictors for cycles where they were not public in time.

No 2026 realized election outcomes exist at this cutoff. Official contemporary EIA/BLS data can be joined for a future real-time forecast at a fixed cutoff, but the literature and this audit do not imply an expected party effect or prediction for 2026.

## Correction to the initial cutoff audit (2026-10-24)

The preceding section overgeneralized from the field name/documentation to the values in the legacy CSV. Direct inspection shows the CSV contains **no 2020 or 2024 rows**. Its 2018 `cpi_yoy_oct` value is **2.5**, which matches BLS’s October 2018 year-over-year CPI, released Nov. 14 after the Nov. 6 election; this is a concrete cutoff problem for that record. Its 2022 value is **8.2**, which matches September 2022 CPI, released Oct. 13 before the Nov. 8 election; October CPI was **7.7**, published Nov. 10 after the election. So the 2022 record appears to use the timely September value despite the `_oct` name. Compare the [CSV](</home/hayden/projects/wethervane-redesign-20261006/integration/data/raw/fundamentals/midterm_history.csv>), [BLS October 2018 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_11142018.htm), [BLS September 2022 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_10132022.htm), and [BLS October 2022 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_11102022.htm).

Therefore, **do not claim all recent rows leak** and do not call every October-labeled observation post-election input without checking its numeric source/vintage. The evidence supports one clear recent lookahead case (2018), one apparently timely 2022 value, and no 2020/2024 rows to audit. The historical table still needs a row-by-row source-and-release-date reconstruction before it is suitable for strict forecast-cutoff evaluation. Any September value should be recorded with its actual observation month and release timestamp, not silently passed off as October CPI.

## Source and horizon correction (2026-10-24)

Kim & Yang’s primary SAGE record confirms the full title, authors Sung Eun Kim and Joonseok Yang, and publication in *American Politics Research* 50(3), pages 312–319 (2022; online first 2021). Its abstract supports only the narrow presidential-election association described above: longer average driving times corresponded to greater accountability for gas-price increases. It does not validate a congressional-midterm effect or the chosen 8-week window. [SAGE article](https://doi.org/10.1177/1532673X211043445).

The earlier proposed gas specification incorrectly compared an 8-week gasoline change with a 12-month CPI change while calling it a relative-price shock. That is not a matched-horizon contrast. The corrected research design treats the 8-week gas-price change as its own predictor interacted with pre-election commuting exposure; CPI, if included, is a separate 12-month predictor. A relative-price feature would need both series measured over the same dates and a stated scaling/deflation rule before it is considered. This correction changes the proposal, not any fit or result.
